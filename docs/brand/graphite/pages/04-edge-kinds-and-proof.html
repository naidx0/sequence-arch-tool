Sheet 04 · The board

Edges: what they mean, and how sure we are

An edge answers two questions at once, and they must never be asked in the same channel. How sure are we? is a claim about the world — it takes the stroke. What kind of call is this? is an identity — it takes the label. Sequence spent seven hues answering the second question and had nothing left to answer the first, so a board full of confident colour could not tell you which of its lines anyone had actually verified.

This is the one place on the board where colour is permitted at all, and the exemption is narrow: it covers the drawn connector and nothing else that touches it.

04.1

Why a connector may carry colour when chrome may not

law 1, and its boundary

Law 1's targets are dividers, headings, section rules, borders and panel edges — the furniture a reader looks past. A drawn connector between two nodes is not furniture. It is the data itself, the way a chart series is, and refusing it colour would be like refusing a chart a second line. That is the whole argument, and it does not extend one pixel further: the plaque's border stays --edge, the card's border stays --edge, the field's dots stay --edge.

Departure, declared. Every other surface in this book obeys "chrome is never coloured" without exception. The board's connectors are the one sanctioned exemption, and they are held to the namespace split in 04.2 in exchange for it. Anything that is not a connector on the canvas — including the connector's own label plaque — is chrome and gets no hue.

A coloured divider and a coloured verdict are the same signal to someone scanning, and the divider wins by volume. This is the shape of the shipped defect: on the default layered arch layout (archLayout.ts:105, 'elk.direction': 'RIGHT') datastores and topics pile on the right, so the downstream half of every board read as a wall of green and amber that means passed and warning everywhere else in the product. Not every board flows that way — graph/layout.ts:114 lays the plain-English board out DOWN — and the pile-up is the point rather than the compass bearing: a layered layout collects sinks at the far end of whichever axis it runs on.

04.2

The namespace split — binding

proof takes the stroke · identity takes the label

Two vocabularies, two channels, and no overlap. Proof state — traced, on-flow, declared-but-unconfirmed — is a claim about the world, so it takes the claim tokens and it takes the stroke. Family or protocol — http, grpc, queue, db, import — is an identity, so it takes the label plaque and it never touches the stroke. A reader should be able to answer "has anyone checked this?" without knowing a single protocol, and answer "what protocol is this?" without knowing a single colour.

04.3

The three proof states

traced · on-flow · declared-but-unconfirmed

Traced is the analyzer following a real call in the source. It is the majority of every board and the reference the other two are read against, so it takes --viz-baseline — which is exactly what that token means on a chart. On-flow is this edge being part of the flow currently played back or selected; selection is the product's own voice, so it takes --accent. Declared is a claim lifted from config or a manifest that the source could not confirm; it is --unknown, and it is broken because the evidence is.

Two of the three resolve to the same neutral. --viz-baseline and --unknown are the same value in both themes, so a board at rest carries no hue at all: traced and declared are separated entirely by the dash, and the only colour that ever appears on this plane is the flow the reader asked for. That is not a collision to be fixed. It is the hue budget in its strongest form — one claim, one hue, and nothing else on the canvas competing with it.

04.4

A claim nobody could check has not failed at anything

--unknown, never --wont

The shipped board strokes a declared edge with the token that means won't fit, failed, removed, blocked. That is the wrong sentence. A service manifest naming a dependency the analyzer could not find in the source is not a failure — it is an open question, and the product has a token for open questions that says so in words: --unknown, "not known, and refusing to guess". Red is spent on failure, and it is only alarming while it stays spent there.

Do
gRPC ‹method›

Grey, broken, and pointed. The reader learns three true things: something claims this call exists, the source did not confirm it, and it is still worth drawing.

Don't
gRPC ‹method›

The shipped .arch-rf-edge-declared, drawn as it actually ships — product.css sets marker-end: none on it, so there is no arrowhead. Two defects, not one. The red reads as this call is broken to every reader who has seen the same red on a failed gate, and it burns the one hue the product cannot afford to make ordinary. And the missing head silently drops direction from the one stroke whose whole content is a claim that A calls B: nothing arrived, so nothing points.

There is no fourth stroke. An edge the analyzer proved absent is not drawn faintly — it is not drawn. Absence is the index rail's job, not the canvas's.

04.5

The arrowhead

does anything land · do we know which way

The head is not identity and does not try to be: five families share two head shapes, so no reader can decode a family from a head. It answers a narrower question that is genuinely a claim about the world. A closed head means something lands here and, for a request, comes back. An open head means nothing lands — the message is published into a lane and the publisher does not wait. No head means the direction was never established, which is the honest picture for db_access: the analyzer saw the table touched and could not say whether it was read or written.

closed · something lands here
open · nothing lands — published into a lane
none · direction not established

The third line is the ruling this book owed: Graphite has no grammar for an arrow we refuse to point, so we do not point one. Drawing heads at both ends would assert two readings, neither of which was measured; drawing one would pick a winner at random.

04.6

The label plaque

.edgetag · 16px · mono --t-10

Identity lives here and nowhere else. The plaque is 16px tall to match .prov, sets in mono at --t-10 because it is a value rather than a label, and carries the board ground --nest-0 so it punches a clean hole in the line it sits on. Its border is --edge: the plaque is chrome, even though the line through it is not.

What the plaque never carries

Two standing prohibitions, both of them things the shipped board does today.

POST /charge yes · the identity, in words
POST /charge no · a hue on chrome
POST /charge · ‹confidence› no · a calibration float

Confidence is a five-rung calibration ladder, and a reader cannot act on the third decimal place of one. It resolves to a provenance tag in the index rail and never renders as a number on the canvas.

Below the floor there is no plaque

The shipped plaque sets below Graphite's smallest size. It is not shrunk further — it is withheld.

The type floor is --t-10, and even that is reserved for mono uppercase. A plaque at the shipped size renders at roughly a fifth of that once the camera is zoomed out to fit a scanned repository, which is not small type — it is texture. Below ‹label zoom floor› the plaque is not drawn at all; hover or selection brings it back at full size, un-clipped.

04.7

Routing

orthogonal elbows · fanned sides · detour, never cross

An edge leaves a card on a stub, turns at right angles in a lane, and enters the far card on a second stub. The exit side is the dominant axis between the two card centres, not a fixed left-to-right: an edge that has to travel upward leaves through the top. Several edges sharing one side are fanned along it rather than stacked on one point, and lanes are held apart so two turns never lie on top of each other. A route that would cut through a third card detours into the lane beside it — a line crossing a card reads as a line touching that card, which is a claim the graph never made.

No corner arc

A turn is a right angle softened by the stroke's own round join.

The radius ramp governs boxes, not paths. Giving a connector an arc radius would put a seventh number on a six-step ramp and make every corner a value someone has to maintain. .edgeline already sets stroke-linejoin: round, so the corner softens by exactly half the stroke width and tracks the stroke automatically — a declared edge at --w-edge and an on-flow edge at --w-flow turn differently, correctly, for free.

04.8

On-flow is a mode, not a third resting state

the board recedes · --board-dim-opacity

Traced and declared are how an edge sits when nobody is looking at it in particular. On-flow is never one of two things a reader has to tell apart at rest, because it only exists while a flow is selected or played back — and while it exists, everything that is not on the flow is at --board-dim-opacity. Selection outranks every other carrier on this canvas and must be more visible than what it replaces, never less.

Three cards and two edges are on the flow; the publish to order.placed is not, so it and its card recede together. Dimming fires on playback and explicit path-focus only, never on an ordinary single-node click.

04.9

The greyscale check

what survives when the hue is taken away

The board's binding invariant is that meaning survives with all colour removed. Below, the same three states drawn achromatically: only dash, width and head remain. Traced and declared lose nothing, because they were never separated by hue in the first place — the dash was always doing all of the work. On-flow and traced are the pair that separate on width alone at rest, and width is the weakest channel in the whole system: it snaps to 1px on a 1× display.

traced · solid · --w-edge
on-flow · solid · --w-flow
declared · --flow-dash-declared · --w-edge
publish · open head · --w-edge
direction unknown · no head · --w-edge

Rows one and two are the residual: at rest, achromatically, they are one line drawn at two weights. The design resolves it by never asking them to be told apart at rest — on-flow exists only while the rest of the board is dimmed, so the reader separates them by what is around the line, not by the line. That is a real dependency and it is written down here rather than discovered later.

Handback. A fourth dash token --flow-dash-onflow would let on-flow stand alone achromatically, and there is a good case for it — a march or a longer rhythm reads as this is the path without borrowing the field's dimming. It is not invented here: inventing a token in a sheet is exactly the failure this book exists to prevent. Raised for the substrate to rule on.

Sheet 06 · The board

Selection, focus, and dimming the rest

Selection is the one thing on the board the reader chose, so it is the one thing that must win the eye. It ADDS — a fill, a ring at full strength, a bounding rect — and it never swaps a border for a quieter one. Everything else on this sheet exists to keep that promise: handles only where a drag changes a stored value, a marquee that reads as a drag and not as a verdict, and a dimming floor that recedes the board without pushing a single title below legibility.

Selection and focus are both the product's own voice, so both are --accent and neither can be mistaken for a verdict. Dimming is not a selection effect at all. It belongs to playback, to explicit path-focus, and — by Decision 6 — to explicit selection, which recedes everything not on the selected node's path.

Decision 6 also gives every card a status dot: small, left of the title, carrying path and selection state in light hues. It amends Decision 2 in one direction only — kind is still never carried by hue alone, but state may now spend a light hue.

Three states and two actual hues; the third is grey on purpose. The dot never appears without its other carriers — a selected node still takes fill, border, ring and elevation (§06.2), and an on-path node's edges are still promoted before any card is dimmed (§06.6). Greyscale loses both hues and no claim: the word beside the dot, the ring and the promoted path say the same things. A kind has no dot, ever — that way lies Decision 2's defect wearing a smaller shape.

The agent is the one kind these three tables describe by effect rather than by declaration, and the difference is recorded here rather than discovered later. Its silhouette is a clip-path chamfer, and neither a box-shadow nor a box-shadow ring can follow a clip — both are painted on the border box, so both would draw a square corner where the card has a cut one. So the agent gets the same three carriers by other means: elevation as --e1-drop and --e2-drop on its outer plate, which are drop-shadow() at the offsets and blurs of --e1 and --e2; and the ring as a thickening of its own drawn outline to --w-rule, in --board-select-edge, which is the identical 2px accent claim made on the real shape instead of on a rectangle around it. Selection still adds, and it is still louder. Nothing about the rule changes; one card draws it with a different instrument, and a book that did not say so would be a book its own substrate contradicts.

06.2

Selection adds. It never swaps.

the ring is --w-rule at full strength

The defect this rule exists to kill is a border-colour swap: the selected card took a new border and the new border was fainter than the resting one, so picking a node made it recede. A swap is a trade — you lose one carrier to gain another. Selection cannot afford a trade, because it is the loudest claim the reader can make on the board. It gets the accent at full strength, not a wash of it, and it keeps everything the resting card already had.

The bounding rect is --w-hair dashed --accent at --r-18, inset by --sp-4 from the --r-14 node it encloses — 18 − 4 = 14, which is the module wall's arithmetic to the pixel, not merely a rung above it. Radii nest correctly because the inset is derived from the two radii, never chosen by eye; a rect drawn at the same radius with a wider inset would look close and be wrong, and this sheet used to draw exactly that. It is dashed because it is a boundary drawn around things rather than a thing that stands there, exactly as --arch-style-module is dashed.

06.3

Handles, and where they are allowed

a handle that does nothing is a lie

Resize handles appear where a drag changes a value the graph actually stores. A module wall has an area the reader lays out, so it has eight. A node card does not: its width is --arch-card-w and its height is content-driven with --arch-card-min-h as a floor, so there is nothing a drag could write. Drawing handles on it would offer a gesture the product cannot honour, and the reader finds that out only by trying.

Four corners and four edge midpoints, each --sp-8 square on --surface-2 with a --w-hair --accent border, centred on the boundary.

The corner marks are the bounding rect's own, and they are drawn so they cannot be read as the wall's grips on the left. A grip is a filled --sp-8 square on --surface-2, sitting astride the boundary with half its body on each side, and a pointer can take it. Connection nubs are not selection. The shipped board painted eight Handle nubs per node while connections were disabled — sixty-four dots on an eight-node graph, none of them draggable. Nubs belong to draw mode and appear only when a connection can actually be made. They are not the resize grips above and they never share their shape.

06.5

Focus is not selection

a gapped double ring, never a fill

Keyboard focus says this is where the next keystroke lands. Selection says this is what I chose. They coexist constantly — arrowing across a board moves focus through cards that are not selected — so they must be told apart at a glance. Both are --accent, because both are the product's voice and neither may be mistaken for a verdict. The difference is geometry: selection is FLUSH and FILLED, focus is a double ring with a visible gap and no fill at all.

Focus is never removed and never restyled away. When both states land on one card the fill reads first and the gapped ring reads second, which is the correct order: what you chose, then where the keyboard is.

Two handbacks live in this one specimen. First, the focus ring's inner band must be the ground it sits on: .forcefocus and :focus-visible both write 0 0 0 2px var(--bg-base) for the gap, which is right everywhere in the app except the board, whose ground is --nest-0. Second, a ring delivered as box-shadow cannot be layered onto a card that already owns its box-shadow: .node declares --e1 later in the sheet and simply replaces the ring, and .node.sel outranks it outright. The three cards above are therefore drawn with the ring on a wrapper. The fix is for the node's shadow to be a composed list — elevation, then selection ring, then focus ring — rather than three rules competing for one property.
06.6

Dimming, and the floor it may not cross

--board-dim-opacity = --st-archived-opacity

Dimming is expensive. It touches every object the reader did not ask about, and spent carelessly it stops meaning anything. Dimming is for playback, for explicit path-focus, and — by Decision 6 — for explicit selection. Selecting a node asks a question about that node, and the board answers by receding everything not on its path, exactly as path-focus does; the dim extends today's flow-playback behaviour to the selected card rather than inventing a second mechanism. Hover still dims nothing, and a marquee drag never does.

The floor is legibility, and it does not move for selection. Dimmed text must still hold 4.5:1 against what is behind it, which rules out fading a title toward the ground. So dimming works in this order: the PATH IS PROMOTED first — its edges take --flow-onflow at --w-flow while everything else stays at the thinner --w-edge, and the on-path cards take their status dots (§06.1) — and only then is the off-path card receded to --board-dim-opacity. Contrast is won by making the answer louder, not by making the rest illegible.

Edges carry the focus before cards do

on the path · .e-onflow · --w-flow
off the path · .e-traced · --w-edge
unconfirmed · .e-declared · --unknown

Decision 6 points declared and traced connectors at the blue flow accent: both carry the accent family while a path is being read, so the graph's flow reads as one continuous blue route rather than grey wiring, and weight, dash and the promoted path's wider stroke still say which claim is which — the distinction survives greyscale on weight alone. What stays out of connectors is every verdict hue: an unconfirmed claim changes weight and dash, never colour into red or green. Focus changes what you are reading, never what is true.

One more rule, and it is the reason dimming is rationed: dimming is never how the board answers a question. If a path is worth focusing, the path is promoted and named. Receding forty cards to make one legible is a way of admitting the one was never loud enough on its own.

06.7

The tokens this sheet spends

every one an alias · no new hue

Selection, focus and dimming spend --accent and a neutral; Decision 6 adds the state dot and the blue connector route, which bring exactly one further hue, --info, already in the palette. Not one verdict hue appears on this sheet outside the .do and .dont marks, which is the point: a selected node and a failing node must never be confusable, and they cannot be if selection has no access to the verdict palette.
